Choose a web developer in this order: decide what the site must do, set a budget, pick the kind of provider, and only then compare people. Judge them on work they have shipped, on whether your company will own every account and file, on what happens after launch, and on the contract.

## 1. Write a one-page brief

Send the same brief to every developer, so the quotes are comparable. It says:

- **The job.** Quotation requests, catalogue downloads, credibility before a tender, or recruitment.
- **The pages.** A list is enough. Ten pages is a different project from forty.
- **Who updates it, and how often.** Weekly posts need an editor your team can use; twice a year may not.
- **What it connects to.** A CRM, WhatsApp, a booking system, an ERP, or e-invoicing.
- **Who writes the words and takes the photos.** Content is the most common reason a project runs late.

## 2. Budget for the build and the years after

A website has a build cost and a running cost (domain, hosting, licences, updates, and small changes). Over five years the running cost is often more than the build. Typical prices, and how to add them up, are in [[How much does a website cost in Malaysia?]].

Give developers a range. Without one, you get quotes for three different projects.

## 3. Pick the kind of provider

A package shop, a freelancer, an agency, or an in-house hire: the choice depends more on what happens after launch than on the build. [[Freelancer, agency or in-house: who should build and run your website?]] compares them.

## 4. Read portfolios on the live sites

1. **Open the sites on your phone.** Check the speed, that the forms work, and that the copyright year is current.
2. **Look for companies like yours.** A B2B distributor needs a catalogue and quotation requests, not a restaurant's booking widget.
3. **Ask which parts they did.** Design, writing, development, or hosting. A site whose design the client supplied tells you less about the developer.
4. **Ask for a reference from a client they have looked after for over a year.** A site shows its quality in its second year more than on launch day.
5. **Check the dates.** Sites from 2019 that nobody has touched since show you only what they did in 2019.

## 5. Settle ownership before anything else

Your company should be the owner of record for the domain, the DNS, the hosting, the website's admin, analytics, and Search Console. The developer gets its own login, which you can remove. This is free to get right at the start and expensive to fix later. The full list is in [[Website ownership checklist: who should hold your domain, hosting and analytics]].

Then check who owns the work itself:

- **Copyright.** Under Malaysia's [Copyright Act 1987](https://www.wipo.int/wipolex/en/text/496686), section 26(2), copyright in a commissioned work is deemed transferred to the person who commissioned it, subject to any agreement between the parties. Look for a clause in the contract that keeps it with the developer.
- **Licences.** Themes, plugins, fonts, and stock photos are licensed, not owned. Ask whose name the licences are in.

This isn't legal advice; if a lot rides on it, ask a lawyer.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> Prefer the developer who answers the ownership and exit questions clearly over the one with the best portfolio. You can see the design. You can't see the account structure until you need it.

## 6. Know what happens after launch

Before you sign, find out:

- how long the free support period lasts, and what it covers;
- what maintenance costs afterwards, what it includes, and how many hours of changes;
- how fast they reply, and how fast they fix;
- how you leave: what they hand over, in what form, and for what fee.

[[What a website maintenance plan should include]] goes through it line by line.

## 7. Check the contract says what you are buying

A good quotation is precise enough that two people would agree on whether it was delivered. It lists:

- the pages, features, and integrations;
- the rounds of revisions, and what counts as one;
- who supplies the words and images, and by when;
- payments tied to stages, not everything up front;
- an acceptance period for each stage;
- what isn't included;
- the exit terms: notice, handover, and what you get.

The warning signs are in [[Red flags in a website or IT quotation]]. Twenty questions to ask your shortlist, with good and bad answers, are in [[Questions to ask a web developer before you sign]].

## Code the client can read

I rebuilt a 24-page site for a Malaysian education and migration consultancy group, and its code is in a public repository. That isn't right for every company, but it means the client, and any developer they hire after me, can see exactly what they own. You get the same result with the code in an account your company controls, or a copy you hold.
