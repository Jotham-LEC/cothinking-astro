This series is for the owner of a new company who also runs its IT. It starts before the first laptop is bought. Two companies are in mind throughout: a six-person accounting firm with an office, and a four-person online retailer whose staff work remotely. I name the providers I would use; I earn nothing from any of them.

## Why boring IT is a good thing

A boring setup is one that rarely demands attention. It uses mainstream, well-documented tools, holds every account in the company's name, and depends on no single person. When something goes wrong, the company recovers in hours rather than weeks.

The alternative is common in small companies:

- Passwords are kept on sticky notes, in a notebook, or in one person's memory.
- The domain was registered by a relative or a former web designer, in their own name.
- Customers' details are held on a salesperson's personal WhatsApp account.
- The only copy of the accounts is on a single office computer.

Each is cheap to avoid while the company is small, and expensive to fix once it has grown.

## Five rules

1. **Hold everything in the company's name.** The domain, the email suite, the bank accounts, and every administrator account belong to the company, and at least two people can access each. See [[Website ownership checklist: who should hold your domain, hosting and analytics]].
2. **Adopt a password manager on day one.** It removes most password resets and shared-login problems, and setting it up is cheapest now. See [[Setting up IT for a small company, Part 3: Password managers]].
3. **Work in the cloud, and back it up.** Prefer the cloud edition of any software to a server in the office, and keep a copy outside it. See [[Setting up IT for a small company, Part 6: Backups|Part 6]].
4. **Choose mainstream tools,** so the next employee or provider can take over without help.
5. **Write it down.** Record who has access to what, where each account is held, and how to regain access.
