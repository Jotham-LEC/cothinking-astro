The biggest red flags in a website or IT quotation are accounts in the vendor's name, a scope too vague to check, promises with no number, and no way out. They don't prove the vendor is dishonest; they mean the document won't help you when something goes wrong. The flags below run roughly from most to least costly.

## 1. Accounts and licences in the vendor's name

"Free domain and hosting for the first year" is common; one Malaysian agency published "FREE 1 year domain, hosting and SSL", renewing at RM699 a year (checked in October 2026; name withheld, source held on file). The deal can be fair. What matters is whose account the domain is in: domain, hosting, email admin, analytics, and licences should be registered to and paid by your company. See the [[Website ownership checklist: who should hold your domain, hosting and analytics]].

## 2. A scope you can't check

"Corporate website, 5 to 8 pages, responsive, SEO-friendly" describes every website. A real scope names the pages, says who writes the text and supplies the photos (the two items most often missing), specifies the features, and states how many rounds of revisions are included. For IT support, look for the list of exclusions. [[How much does a website cost in Malaysia?]] covers the costs outside a scope.

## 3. "Unlimited", or numbers without definitions

Nothing is unlimited; the limit is unwritten, so the vendor sets it when it matters. Ask for a number. A "4-hour response" may be an automatic email (see [[How to choose an IT support provider for a small company]]).

## 4. No exit

The contract should state the notice period, what you get when you leave (files, code, credentials, a list of accounts), how quickly, and any fee. Watch for uncapped auto-renewal and long minimum terms: a few months is reasonable, three years isn't.

## 5. Lock-in by the platform

One popular website builder states that its sites run only on its own servers. That can suit a business that wants to edit its own site, but the quotation should say so. Worse is an agency's own system that only it can edit and host. Ask: "If we stop paying you, can we take the site elsewhere, and in what form?"

- **Good:** "It's WordPress; here are the export and the files", or "It's in a code repository in your account".
- **Bad:** "You wouldn't want to."

## 6. No backups, or untested ones

The quotation should say how often the site and database are backed up, where, for how long, and how a restore is done. Malaysia's National Cyber Security Agency advises copies "on a separate media and stored offline at an alternate site" ([NACSA](https://nacsa.gov.my/advisory9.php)). No mention may mean no backups.

## 7. All the money up front

A deposit is normal; full payment before delivery removes your leverage. Tie payments to milestones you can accept: a design approved, a site on a staging address, a launch.

## 8. No acceptance step

Without one, "done" is whatever the vendor says. You need a set number of days to review each phase and a way to list what is unfinished.

## 9. Prices that don't add up

Watch for a low build price with a high monthly fee, a package missing pages you need, licences renewing in someone else's name, and tax left unstated. Ask for the total of the first two years, because that is the figure to compare.

> [!TIP] Treat a red flag as a question
> Ask about it in writing. A good vendor fixes the quotation; one who resists putting your domain in your name has answered for you.

## What a clear quotation contains

1. Both legal names, the vendor's SSM number, and a validity date.
2. The scope: pages or systems, features, content and who supplies it, and revisions.
3. Exclusions, and how excluded work will be priced (if by the day, how long a day is).
4. Deliverables by phase, with dates and an acceptance period.
5. A payment schedule tied to those phases, and whether tax is included.
6. Ownership of every account, licence, design, and the code once paid.
7. Hosting, backups, and security, if included, with specifics.
8. Support after launch (see [[What a website maintenance plan should include]]).
9. Renewal, notice period, and handover on exit.

One page can hold all of this.

## What I have seen

I rebuilt a 24-page site for a Malaysian education and migration consultancy group in a public code repository, so the client sees every change and a new developer would start where I would. Most sites shouldn't be public, but ask the same of any quotation: if we part ways, what do you leave with?
