> What follows is the script of my first video essay, written to be spoken rather than read. It keeps the outline I recorded from and the conversational, unpolished tone of a talk, so it reads more like a rant than an essay.

- Hey there, Jotham here.
- Today, we're going to talk about why you should still learn how to code, despite AI supposedly taking over programming jobs.
- This video is meant for people who have "career remorse" — mostly fresh graduates or students studying computer science or programming, who might be worried that the time they've spent building this career has gone to waste, or that they've picked the wrong career altogether.
- You'll find that seasoned developers are less likely to share these concerns — and for good reason, which I'll explain later in the video.
- Without further ado, let's dive in.

## AI is not an expert

- First of all, AI is great at building stuff, but it's not exactly an expert. Maybe it'll get there someday — but even then, someone still has to define what "good" looks like.
  - The ability to write code is not what programming is all about.
    - Programming is about achieving a particular outcome through problem solving.
    - Sometimes, it involves writing custom code, or perhaps using a small library, or even leveraging existing open-source tools. Instead of building from scratch, why not use what's already available?
    - The point is, in programming, there are many different ways to solve the same problem. None of them are necessarily wrong, but some are better than others. It involves a whole series of tiny decisions that eventually add up to become an entire product or solution.
    - Each method comes with its own benefits, trade-offs, costs, time commitments and so on.
  - You can see it in a typical programmer's day-to-day. A large part of their day involves making decisions.
    - They sit in product meetings to understand their user base and decide on features. They identify friction, and triage and fix bugs. And each bug can be fixed in multiple ways, each with its own pros and cons.
    - What separates a seasoned developer from someone new is not the lines of code they write. It's the ability to understand the project's needs and make good judgement calls — making the right decisions at the right time. And that's called expertise, or wisdom.
    - And this expertise is cultivated by working with many different teams, being exposed to many codebases, and having strong fundamentals in what their particular framework or language can or cannot do.
  - You can get AI to make the decisions on your behalf, but it'll inevitably make subpar decisions that you won't agree with. Or worse, ones that are totally wrong for your use case.
    - And the worst part is — the subpar code works, but only in the narrow, predefined way that you specified in your prompts.
    - But with the right expertise, you can steer AI towards better outcomes.
      - You'll notice that seasoned developers prompt differently from juniors, and the outcomes are different as well. AI becomes a force multiplier for the expertise they already have.
      - Which is why I'm not totally against AI — it's merely a powerful tool. A Formula One car is fast, but it needs a good driver to get the most out of it. And good drivers are always in demand, and highly paid.
      - And that's why seasoned developers are less worried about AI than juniors are.
  - Another way of thinking about it is that a programming language is a translation layer — a way of translating the decisions and intended outcomes in your head into something a computer can run and execute.
    - You might argue that with agentic AI, English has now become the universal programming language. AI can convert your intentions into code that machines can run — and you'd be correct.
    - But English is a poor programming language. It's open to multiple interpretations, and means different things in different contexts. At its core, it's inconsistent and imprecise.
    - "Oh... if only there were a way to get a machine to perform exactly the way I imagine it in my head." — Well, welcome to programming.
  - Most people build this expertise through years of working experience, but as a student or fresh graduate, you don't have that. So learning is your next best thing — lectures, reviewing codebases, or working on university capstone projects. Either way, you'll end up being a better developer than someone who has given up on learning and is overly reliant on AI.
    - And I'm not going to sugarcoat it — the junior market is tough right now. It's unfair, because graduates today have to work harder than previous generations just to stand out, competing not only with other applicants, but with AI as well.
    - But you're not entirely helpless either. There are always options, approaches you can take and things you can do to land your first job.
    - My point is, AI does the job well, but it can't replace expertise.

## Programming is fun

- The second reason why you should keep learning programming is simply because it's fun.
  - I think many people have been focusing a lot on programming the career, rather than programming the activity itself.
  - Programming is like a mix between a sandbox and a puzzle game — and learning how to code is learning the game mechanics.
  - Using AI to complete the puzzle for you takes the joy out of the game itself.
  - Which is a shame, because programming is one of the few activities where you get almost immediate feedback — the code either works, or it doesn't.
    - In other jobs like marketing, I won't know whether a campaign performs well until weeks after it goes live.
    - Programming also happens in a controlled environment. If the device, the dependencies, the tools and the codebase are the same, the product will produce the same results every single time.
    - My work as a career coach is different. Some strategies work well for some clients but not for others. Even when my clients achieve their goals, I have no idea how much my coaching contributed to that. It could be 10%, 50% or nothing at all. Sometimes a client gets a promotion simply because their boss was in a good mood, and they were lucky. I know that coaching works, simply because my clients say so. But many of the factors are unknowable and uncontrollable, and that comes with the job.
  - But that's not the case with programming — the fact that it provides near-immediate feedback in a controlled environment means that it can be gamified.
    - In fact, if you follow a test-driven development workflow, it's like completing a quest log.
  - And no one knows this better than [Boot.dev](https://www.boot.dev). I believe they've made programming more fun than any other course I've come across so far. (Not sponsored.)
    - Every lesson comes with a simple assignment and an in-browser IDE that gives you immediate feedback on whether your code passes or fails the tests.
    - When I get stuck, I can use the in-game currency to view the solution.
    - If I get past the assignment without help, I get to review the solution for free — and it's interesting to compare my solution with theirs and learn new ways to approach the same problem.
    - They have a built-in AI system that is specifically instructed not to give the solution outright, but to nudge me with pointers and questions, so I figure it out by myself — with help.
  - Of course, actual programming is not like Boot.dev or coding tutorials.
    - There are no daily quests... no coding streaks... and even though my code might be "correct" because it runs without bugs, there's no sure way to know whether it's "proper" without consulting a senior dev.
    - But AI can get you some of the way there — it can serve as a guide, or even help gamify your coding project, similar to how Boot.dev does it.
    - My point is — for many people, AI has taken the joy out of programming, but realistically, nothing is stopping you from using AI to make programming more fun and rewarding instead.
  - In cooking terms, AI is like a Thermomix. It trivialises cooking and makes life easy. And yet, many people prefer cooking the proper way, with a stove, a pan, a knife and a chopping board. Many choose to make ingredients from scratch rather than buy them pre-made — because convenience is not the same as fun.
  - It means something for a chef to develop a relationship with their tools and ingredients, and with the art of cooking.
  - And chefs are still in demand — good ones are highly paid, despite cooking being more convenient and easy than ever before. So why should developers be any different? Plus, a chef knows how to use a Thermomix far better than someone who can't cook — the same way a seasoned developer knows how to use AI far better than someone who can't code.

## You don't really have a choice

- My next point — why I think you should still learn programming despite AI — is simply that you don't really have a choice.
  - It's important to note that AI didn't just affect tech jobs — it affected white-collar jobs across the board, from writers, designers and marketers to, more recently, mathematicians too.
  - Your other option is to venture into industries that are relatively cushioned from AI disruption, but many of those jobs are low-paying, require you to travel long distances, work in dangerous conditions, or take on a lot of risk.
    - For instance — working as an electrician in data centres, which should be a booming industry.
    - Sure, the pay is good, but are you willing to relocate to the middle of nowhere, work long hours, handle sensitive equipment and be constantly on call in case things break?
  - And keep in mind — there's a cost to switching careers, and it pays to spend some time considering it.
  - Compared to that, being a developer is comfortable. You:
    - Work sensible hours.
    - Can work remotely, which means no commuting and no dressing up.
    - Earn a decent wage.
    - And if money is a problem, you have relatively more opportunities to moonlight, take on freelance work, or build hobby projects.
    - You also have some control over your work environment — from your tooling and operating system to ergonomic keyboards and whatnot.
  - Even with AI disruption, dev work is still a cushy job. If you had an easy, obvious alternative, you'd probably have taken it already.
  - Chances are... you've already made the choice to commit years of your life to pursuing this career, and for good reasons.
  - Perhaps you're good with computers, or someone in your family is in the industry and you think their work is cool, or something. I don't know you personally, but chances are, AI didn't really change what got you into this industry to begin with.
  - And if you have no plans to pivot your career entirely, and you don't really have any good alternatives — rather than getting stuck in limbo, not making a decision at all...
  - Why not stick to the existing decision you've made, and commit to it fully?
  - Now, it may feel like committing fully to this career is like driving yourself into a corner — or even off a cliff.
  - But from personal experience, and plenty of anecdotal evidence, being passionate and serious about a subject generally opens more doors to new and better opportunities rather than closing them. These doors may involve programming, they may not — but you'll benefit regardless.
  - It may seem like the decision you make today will impact you for the rest of your life. And you're right, to a certain extent — but also realise that your destiny is not fixed.
  - At any point, you can choose to pivot to something else, and it doesn't have to be now. And if you're keeping up with AI advancements — which you most probably are — you'll know when it's time to jump ship, before it's too late. And from what I can tell, we're not at that point just yet.
  - My point is, there's no better option out there, so commit to the one you've already chosen.

## You'll be fine if you lock in

- Which leads to my final point: you're most likely going to be fine if you lock in.
  - Advances in AI will change your relationship with programming, but they don't change your overall strategy for growing your career.
  - Amazing developers have amazing developer skills, sure — but what we respect is not the skills themselves, but the qualities they've embodied to get there.
  - We look up to amazing developers because they have the discipline, the passion for the craft, the critical thinking skills and even the charisma to become who they are today. Their programming skills are merely a by-product of those qualities.
  - Even if these developers had chosen an entirely different career path, they'd most likely end up just as successful.
  - The skills don't matter as much as those qualities.
  - Companies pay high salaries for those qualities. Businesses prefer freelancers with those qualities. You probably subscribe to YouTube channels with those qualities, even if they aren't run by top-tier developers.
  - So I see Reddit posts with memes about quitting the industry to become plumbers and farmers. I see people on the two extreme sides of the pro-AI and anti-AI debate. To me, they're not just arguing about policy or strategy. Fundamentally, they're talking about uncertainty and anxiety.
  - AI is here, it's messing things up right now, and we don't know what's going to happen to us in the future. These are all the same conversation.
  - And the answer to that is relatively simple for ordinary people like us — we just have to lock in. Be diligent, be disciplined, learn as much as you can, and build the qualities that make you a capable, respectable developer. Will the stuff you learn be obsolete in the next three years? Maybe. Will you be wasting your time? Maybe.
  - But I believe that time spent learning and thinking deeply about a subject is never time wasted. Because putting in that effort is hard, and you'll prove to yourself that you're capable of doing hard things.
  - And if you've built those qualities, you'll be fine — no matter where you go.

## So why bother?

- So why should you bother learning programming in the age of AI?
  - You should learn, because although code is cheap, expertise is still expensive.
  - You should learn, because programming is fun, learning is fun, and learning how to program is fun squared.
  - You should learn, because what other choice do you have?
  - Finally, you should learn, because you're going to be fine — you're working towards becoming the best version of yourself.
